Part II

How we teach
(or don't)

There are no "designated" teachers in RS School.

There are three groups of people:

  1. Those who want to become developers.
  2. Those who want to become developers, learn on their own, and help other students learn.
  3. Those who already became developers and help others in their free time.

All mentors are developers from various companies. No one is paid for teaching.

"I never teach my pupils.
I only attempt to provide
the conditions in which they can learn." — Albert Einstein

The school is responsible for the environment: assignments, rhythm, checkpoints, feedback, communication.
You're responsible for the learning process — putting in the time, working through it, finishing.

Tandi

What is Tandi

An observer tool.

  • Collects diaries
  • Tracks progress through checkpoints
  • Helps the coordinator see where teams are
  • Generates periodic feedback
Tandi is a chronicler, not a judge.
Counts to make things visible — not to punish.

This course is the first beyond RS Tandem
where Tandi launches outside its native context.

Part VII

Why a diary

The diary is the central practice of this course.

It's not the code that goes to defence.
It's the human — someone who can reflect on what they're doing.

Before "how" — "why" matters.

A diary — two tools in one

ToolWhat it does
ThinkingWhen you write something in words, you don't retell a finished thought — you build it. Many concepts that felt clear in your head fall apart on paper. That's where real understanding starts.
HistoryIn a month you'll re-read your week-3 entry and notice: "I didn't know then what's obvious now." The most powerful proof of growth there is — your own writing.

Deeper on "the illusion of understanding" — in the Inspiration deck.

Part VIII

How to keep a diary

What it is, technically

  • A Markdown file in the diary/ folder of your repository
  • One entry = one day of work (or a focused session)
  • Minimum: ≥2 entries in W1, then per checkpoint

Filename — important

Format: YYYY-MM-DD.md

e.g. 2026-04-28.md

Also allowed: DD-MM-YYYY, YY-MM-DD with - or .

A file without a date in its name does not count.
(reflection.md, notes.md — no.)

Tandi counts entries by the date in the filename. Typo it — lose the credit.

What to write

  • What you tried to do today
  • Where you got stuck and how you searched for a way out
  • What you newly understood (about Angular, about yourself, about the team)
  • What didn't work and why

DON'T write:

  • A list of commits
  • A retelling of the docs
  • "Today I learned Angular"

More on diary entry levels (0–4) — see the standalone diary.html page

Points are a tool to see progress.
Not the goal.

In 2026 anyone can build a working widget with a single prompt.
That doesn't mean the person learned —
it means AI can.

We need to tell the difference.

What we care about

  • Real knowledge — what stays in your head, not in your chat with an agent
  • Hands-on skills — what you can write, explain out loud, defend
  • Learning path — how you approached the task, where you got stuck, what you got out of it
  • Thinking — how you decompose, how you weigh tradeoffs, how you catch your own mistakes

Mechanics look at the path, not the finished thing

MechanicNOT thisBUT this
Defences"does the final code work""do you understand what and why you wrote"
Diarylist of commits, doc retellingtrace of your thinking — where you got stuck, what you got
Code reviewformality for the checkboxcan you discuss someone else's code as your own
AI is a normal tool. Use it — yes.
Faking understanding through AI — no.
That's self-deception, and it's caught easily at any defence.

Operational rules

  • The diary is a gate, not points by itself. No diary → nothing else counts.
  • Per-user scores are private. Aggregates are visible. Specific scores are visible to the student in their own dashboard.

Part XI

What matters,
what doesn't

What matters

  • You do it with your hands. Not reading about Angular — writing Angular code every day.
  • Functional requirements are met. The button clicks, the form submits.
  • You keep a diary — regularly, with a date in the filename, with reflection, not commit-listing.
  • You can defend your code. Not just "it works" — but "I understand how and why".
  • You help your team — code review, explanations, resolving conflicts in the code.

What does NOT matter (especially at the start)

"School assignments are aimed at meeting functional requirements.
Code quality at the early stage of learning isn't particularly important." — traditional RS School wording
  • Perfect architecture in week one. First "it works" — then "it's nice".
  • Strict code style at the start. The linter will help; learning beauty through practice beats fearing the commit button.
  • 100% test coverage in W1–W2. Tests will come — as a separate practice.
  • Comparing yourself with others. Competition is not the goal.
